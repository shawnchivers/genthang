A 16-bit console you can
hold in one hand.

GenThang Modern is a 3D-printed handheld built around a Tang Nano 20K running GenThang, an open-source FPGA clone of the Sega Genesis / Mega Drive that plays original Genesis games, a 3.5" 640×480 IPS panel and a 1200 mAh battery module. This variant has the classic A · B · C layout.

Beta: this handheld has been printed, assembled and played, but some design questions are still open, such as cooling. Expect changes to the files and steps.

Overview

What goes where

Three printed parts sandwich four layers. The front shell holds the buttons, the button board and the screen stack. The back shell carries the battery, the FPGA board and the audio parts. Everything inside plugs together with Dupont jumpers. The only soldering is headers, the diode, the screen's power lead and the audio filter.

1

Print the three parts

The STLs are already oriented. Don't rotate them, and turn supports off. The front prints face down, and its recesses are 45° bezels, so they grow from the bed like countersinks.

  • PLA or PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 3–4 perimeters, 20–25 % infill (solid screw bosses).
  • Bed: smooth PEI or glass for a glossy face, textured PEI for matte. The face is the first layer, so clean the bed.
  • Elephant-foot compensation 0.15–0.2 mm so the face edge and button holes don't flare.
  • First layer slow (~20 mm/s), 100 % flow. Seam "rear" or aligned, away from the visible face edge.
  • Everything fits a 200 × 200 × 100 mm printer.
2

Flash and prepare the Nano 20K

Flash first, while the board is still bare. Use genthang_nano20k_raw_flash.bin: the raw build reads direct buttons and drives the speaker and jack. A db9 image would read the wrong pins.

  1. Verify the download (sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS --ignore-missing), then write it at 0x000000 with Gowin Programmer: GW2AR-18C, External Flash Mode, exFlash Erase, Program thru GAO-bridge.
  2. Format a microSD card (MBR + FAT32/exFAT) and copy your .md / .bin / .gen ROMs onto it (see getting your games).
  3. Solder a 20-pin right-angle header on each row, on the component side, pins pointing outward like wings.
  4. Trim the legs on the underside to about 1.5 mm, so the board sits flat on its foam pads.
Why wings? They keep every jumper inside the case's 28 mm top section. The Nano needs no zip ties: four corner lands with foam tape hold it, and side guards locate it.
3

Buttons: header, membranes, caps

The six-button board is used as-is. Only its bottom row is open in this face: pads R · B · A become A · B · C. X, Y and L stay unwired under a solid face.

  1. Solder a 13-pin right-angle header on the back of the board, pins pointing down (toward the battery), lying along the board.
  2. Cut one A/B membrane through the rib midway between its domes (8.2 mm from each dome centre). Keep the half with a locating ear.
  3. Front shell face down. The caps go in from the inside: drop the D-pad, the two Start/Select pills and the three A/B caps into their holes, so their little legs (flanges) rest on the inside of the face.
  4. Lay the D-pad rubber over the D-pad, and the single Start/Select rubber (one piece for both) over the two pills.
  5. Lay the intact membrane over B–C, and the half over A with its ear lower right and the cut edge toward the lower left (as drawn).
  6. Lower the board on, check the membranes are still seated, and fit five M1.7 × 5 screws. Leave out the one just below the header, left of centre.
Check the half against B–C. The fit comes from photos; they clear by under 0.5 mm. If they touch, shave a little more off the cut edge, away from the dome.
4

Screen stack

The panel's flex is short, so it S-folds under the driver board. That's why there is extra room at the top of the case: the mini-HDMI plug needs the room above the board.

  1. Slide the glass in from the back, FPC edge down. It has 0.5 mm per side, plus 1.3 mm for the panel's ledge.
  2. Fit the bracket with 4 × M2 × 5: two above the glass, one lower left, one into the right wall. Feed the flex through its 44 mm notch. If your glass is thinner than 4.5 mm, add foam tape of the difference on the bracket.
  3. Fold the flex up the back of the bracket, down, then up into the board's connector. Make gentle folds (~1.5 mm), not creases.
  4. Stick two 6 × 35 mm strips of 1 mm insulating foam tape into the bracket's recesses, then press the board's bare side on, parts facing the back. No pins, no heat staking. Keep tape off the flex path and solder joints.
  5. Plug the ribbon's mini-HDMI end straight down into the board. Solder the JST-PH lead to its USB-C VBUS/GND pads (or use a right-angle USB-C plug).
Don't use thicker tape. It moves the board back into the Nano's HDMI plug. Strain-relieve the HDMI ribbon; don't let the tape carry it.
5

Fill the back shell

The back is a wedge: 35.2 mm at the bottom for the battery and your palms, 28 mm at the top.

  1. PM11, components toward the front (header pins point at you), USB-C and switch out of the bottom edge. Two M3 × 4 through its free holes; locating pins take the holes under the cell.
  2. Nano 20K on its four corner lands with a dab of foam tape each, USB-C out of the top edge, HDMI pointing down.
  3. Speaker into the flat-seated ring upper right, behind the hex grille.
  4. PJ-307 jack upside down in its cradle on the bottom edge, pins up.
  5. Build the audio filter on the 20 × 12 mm perfboard and tape it down upper left.
Battery gauge: a light tube in the front face sits 1 mm over the PM11's four LEDs. Read the charge below Start/Select while you play. There's no low-voltage cut-off, so recharge at one LED.
6

Wire it up

The Nano sits component side forward, so J6 faces the D-pad and the jack, and J5 faces the face buttons. No wire crosses the board. Leave ~8 cm of slack on the button and HDMI leads so the halves open like a book.

  1. Power: PM11 5 V pair 1 → 1N5817 → Nano J5.1 / J5.2. Pair 2 → JST-PH → PCB800850.
  2. Buttons: 10 jumpers from the button header. Positions 9–11 (X Y L) stay empty.
  3. Speaker: solder to the Nano's SPEAKER pads. Jack: 4 jumpers, pins 29, 30, 31 and GND.
  4. HDMI ribbon: run it down the left of the Nano, over the left-wing housings, and fold the spare flat beside it.
7

Close up and power on

  1. Fold the halves together, tucking the HDMI ribbon and jumpers into the side channels. Nothing should pinch.
  2. Fit six M2.3 × 8 from the back. Snug, not tight: they're self-tapping into plastic.
  3. Insert the microSD through the top slot below the Nano's USB-C.
  4. Slide the PM11 switch ON. The menu lists your ROMs. B / C opens, A goes up, Start opens Options.
  5. In game, press Mode to open the menu.
Charging: switch ON and plug USB-C into the bottom edge. The LEDs chase while charging and go solid when full (~1 h). The PM11 is not a UPS: don't plug the charger in mid-game.
Charging turns the console on. The PM11 passes 5 V through to its outputs whenever USB-C is plugged in, even with its switch OFF, so the Nano and screen boot while charging. To charge with the console off you currently have to unplug the 5 V Dupont pairs from the PM11 header.
Proposed fix: add a small DPDT slide switch (e.g. SS22D, 2.54 mm pitch) with one pole in each +5 V Dupont lead (Nano and screen), so both loads are cut. Charging still works with this switch OFF. It needs a slot in the back shell's bottom edge next to the PM11 switch; that isn't in the CAD yet.
Before you start

Notes

Open design questions

The case has been printed and assembled and it plays. Still being worked out: cooling (the FPGA and screen driver sit in a closed shell with no vents yet) and longer-term fit and durability. Keep an eye on heat during long sessions.

Membranes are photo-sized

Their outlines come from photos on grid paper, scaled by the A/B button pitch. Thickness is assumed to be the stock 2.7 mm gap. Check the half membrane against B–C as you assemble.

Check your PM11

The cell's position, the switch lever (~3.8 mm) and the USB-A socket are modelled from photos and NULLLAB's STEP model. The openings are oversized by 1.5 mm to allow for this.

Header order

Wire by position, not by the PCB's silkscreen. The board's "R" pad is A, "B" is B and "A" is C. Confirm pin 1 against your board's silkscreen before plugging in.

Games

Getting your games legally

GenThang plays Sega Genesis / Mega Drive ROM files, but it doesn't come with any. The clean way to get them is to back up cartridges you own yourself. A USB cartridge reader such as the Krikzz FlashKit MD plugs into a PC. Its FlashKit software (Windows; Mono on macOS and Linux) reads the cartridge ROM to a .bin you can copy to the microSD card. It can also back up and restore the cart's save RAM.

Homebrew is the other easy option: many new Genesis games are released free, or sold, by their authors as ROM files. Downloading copies of commercial games you don't own is generally copyright infringement, so stick to your own dumps and homebrew.
